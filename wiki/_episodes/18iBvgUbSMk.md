## Summary
Sleep expert Stephanie Romiszewski explains why common [[concepts/sleep-hygiene]] advice often fails and argues that building consistent [[concepts/habits]] around wake-up time matters more than bedtime rituals. She covers the physiological consequences of poor [[concepts/sleep]] — from [[concepts/cytokines]] and [[concepts/inflammation]] to [[concepts/cognitive-ability]] decline — and advocates [[methods/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-for-insomnia]] and [[methods/sleep-restriction-therapy]] as evidence-based approaches to retrain the sleep drive.

## Key Claims
- [[concepts/anxiety]] and [[concepts/stress]] are the primary drivers of [[concepts/insomnia]] (2:11, 37:46)
- Poor sleep increases [[concepts/cytokines]] and [[concepts/inflammation]], impairing [[concepts/cognitive-ability]] (8:11)
- Sleep supports [[concepts/muscle-repair]] and protects against [[concepts/alzheimer-s]] (8:29, 8:49)
- [[concepts/sleep-debt]] accumulates and cannot simply be "paid off" on weekends (11:15)
- [[concepts/fatigue]] is not the same as [[concepts/sleepiness]]; confusing the two worsens sleep problems (12:09, 21:24)
- Consistency of wake-up time matters more than bedtime (7:20)
- The snooze button is pointless and unsupported by research (5:30)
- [[concepts/light-exposure]] from phones reduces [[concepts/melatonin]] and disrupts sleep drive (9:05)
- [[methods/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-for-insomnia]] retrains the sleep drive more effectively than hygiene tips alone (10:40, 19:51)
- [[concepts/caffeine]] and [[concepts/covid-19]] have significantly disrupted population sleep patterns (28:45, 35:11)
- [[concepts/depression]] and [[concepts/insomnia]] form a bidirectional cycle (19:56, 48:40, 49:05)
- Creating a consistent [[concepts/sleep-opportunity]] window is essential (59:20)
- Managing [[concepts/stress]] and [[concepts/anxiety]] is prerequisite to fixing sleep (57:58, 58:05)
